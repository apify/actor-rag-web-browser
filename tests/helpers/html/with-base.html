The old harbour lies at the mouth of the river, where the fishing boats come in early in the morning. Most of the warehouses along the quay were built in the nineteenth century and now serve as shops, workshops and small galleries. Read the relative link for the history of the place.

A walk along the water takes about an hour and passes the lighthouse, the customs house and the market hall. The market is open every day except Sunday, and the best time to visit is before noon, when the stalls still have fresh fish, bread and vegetables. The in-page anchor leads back up.

The town museum keeps a collection of maps, ship models and photographs of the harbour from the time it was one of the busiest ports on the coast. Guided tours start at the main entrance twice a day, and the museum café on the top floor has a view over the roofs of the old town and the river.

In the summer, ferries leave the harbour every two hours for the islands in the bay. The crossing takes forty minutes, and bicycles can be taken on board for a small fee. Tickets are sold at the kiosk next to the pier, and it's worth buying them a day ahead during the festival week in July.

The cheapest way to reach the harbour is the tram from the railway station, which stops right at the quay. There are a few car parks on the edge of the old town, but the streets around the harbour are closed to traffic on weekends, when the whole quarter turns into a pedestrian zone full of street music.

Several restaurants on the quay serve the catch of the day, usually grilled and with potatoes from the farms up the valley. Tables outside fill up quickly on warm evenings, so booking ahead is a good idea, especially on Fridays, when the brass band plays on the square in front of the old customs house.
